Sketchbook: Travel

Architectural sketching is not simply the recording of images but a deep analytical process in which a line becomes a bridge between perception and professional understanding. By sketching cityscapes, architects learn to “read” layers of the urban fabric, from historical context to the rhythm of contemporary development. Each stroke reveals the proportions of squares, the scale of façades and the dialogue between buildings of different eras, turning abstract plans into tangible reality.

This method develops critical thinking: through visual analysis of neighbourhood density, silhouettes and public areas, a designer learns the language through which cities speak to their residents. Sketching teaches us to see not only objects but also the spaces between them, where public life emerges. Drawings of street scenes, courtyards and transport hubs help us understand how the human body interacts with space, while hand-drawn graphics record this choreography of movement and stillness.

This practice becomes a laboratory for studying successes and failures in urban planning. By reproducing details of historic façades or diagramming new complexes, an architect engages with the logic of different building eras. In an age of digital renders, hand sketching remains a way to preserve a “sense of proportion”—the balance between function, aesthetics and the memory of a city that cannot be reduced to algorithms. It is the art of seeing, thinking and remembering.
